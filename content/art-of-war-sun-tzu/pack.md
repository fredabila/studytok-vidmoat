## About
The Art of War is a short Chinese text on strategy, traditionally credited to the general Sun Tzu
and written roughly 2,500 years ago. Its thirteen chapters are less about fighting than about
avoiding fights you cannot win: knowing yourself and your opponent, choosing the ground, moving
fast, and winning before the battle starts. Managers, coaches, lawyers and students of history
still read it because the ideas transfer to any contest where resources are limited. This pack
explains the core ideas in plain language, with examples from exams, sport and work.

## Units
### Know yourself, know your opponent
The most quoted idea in the book: if you know the enemy and know yourself, you need not fear the
result of a hundred battles. Sun Tzu treats information as the first weapon. Before any move you
assess five things: the cause people are fighting for, the conditions (weather and timing), the
terrain, the leadership, and discipline. For a student this is the difference between "I studied
a lot" and "I know which topics I lose marks on, and which the examiner loves".
- The five factors are listed in Chapter 1 ("Laying Plans"): moral law, heaven, earth, the commander, and method and discipline.
- The "know yourself" line is from Chapter 3 ("Attack by Stratagem").

### The best victory is the one you do not have to fight
Sun Tzu ranks ways of winning. The highest is to defeat the enemy's plans; next is to break their
alliances; next is to beat their army; the worst is to besiege a walled city, which is slow and
costly. The lesson is to spend effort where it changes the outcome most. In a negotiation, that
means fixing the other side's reasons to say no before the meeting. In revision, it means clearing
the misunderstanding that causes a whole class of mistakes rather than redoing twenty exercises.
- The ranking of victories is in Chapter 3.
- The famous line "supreme excellence consists in breaking the enemy's resistance without fighting" is from the same chapter.

### Speed, surprise and the shape of water
Water has no fixed shape; it takes the shape of the ground and flows around the strong points.
Sun Tzu wants an army to do the same: avoid strength, strike weakness, and change plan as the
situation changes. He also warns that long campaigns exhaust a state, so speed matters more than
perfection. The modern version is the agile team that ships a small thing this week rather than a
perfect thing next year, and the exam candidate who answers the easy questions first.
- The water comparison is in Chapter 6 ("Weak Points and Strong").
- Chapter 2 ("Waging War") argues that no country has ever benefited from a long war.

### Terrain and position
Several chapters sort ground into types (open, narrow, high, surrounded) and say what to do on
each: take the high ground, never fight with a river at your back unless you mean to, and leave an
enemy a way out so they do not fight to the death. Position is a resource you choose before the
contest. A debater picks which claims to defend; a chess player trades pieces to reach an endgame
they know; a student picks the seat, the time of day and the environment that make focus easy.
- Chapter 10 ("Terrain") and Chapter 11 ("The Nine Situations") deal with ground.
- "When you surround an army, leave an outlet free" is from Chapter 7 ("Manoeuvring").

### Leading people
Sun Tzu's commander is calm, fair and consistent. Soldiers must be treated well enough to be loyal
and disciplined enough to be reliable; orders must be clear before punishment is fair. He warns
against five faults in a leader: recklessness, cowardice, a quick temper, a delicate sense of
honour that can be shamed into mistakes, and excessive concern for the troops that makes them
soft. Group projects, captaincies and prefect roles meet all five.
- The five dangerous faults of a general are listed in Chapter 8 ("Variation in Tactics").

## Facts
- The text is traditionally attributed to Sun Tzu (Sunzi), a general of the state of Wu, around the 5th century BCE; many scholars think it was compiled over time.
- It has thirteen chapters and is written in classical Chinese.
- The earliest known copies were found in a Han dynasty tomb at Yinqueshan in 1972, written on bamboo strips.
- Lionel Giles's 1910 English translation is public domain and is the one on Project Gutenberg.
- The book was studied by Napoleon's era only through a 1772 French translation by the Jesuit Jean Joseph Marie Amiot.
