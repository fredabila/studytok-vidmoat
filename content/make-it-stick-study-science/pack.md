## About
Most revision advice is folklore. This pack is the small set of findings from cognitive psychology
that hold up in experiment after experiment: testing yourself beats rereading, spacing beats
cramming, mixing topics beats blocking them, and the feeling of fluency is a trap. Each unit
explains one finding, why it works, and how to use it this week. StudyTok's own feed is built on
these ideas, so you will recognise them in how lessons come back.

## Units
### Retrieval beats rereading
Reading your notes again feels productive because the words look familiar. But familiarity is
not memory. Pulling an answer out of your head, with the book closed, is what strengthens it.
In classic experiments students who read a passage once and then tested themselves recalled far
more a week later than students who reread it four times, even though the rereaders felt more
confident. The practical rule: after every lesson, close it and write down three things you
remember, then check.
- Roediger and Karpicke (2006) found that one reading plus self-testing beat repeated reading on a one-week delayed test.
- The effect is called the testing effect or retrieval practice.

### Space it out
Ten minutes a day for six days beats one hour on one day, even though the total time is the same.
Memory consolidates between sessions, and each return forces a little forgetting to be reversed,
which is exactly what makes the trace durable. The ideal gap grows as you learn: a day, then a
few days, then a week or two. Cramming works for tomorrow's test and fails for the exam in June.
- Spaced practice has been replicated since Ebbinghaus's experiments on himself in the 1880s.
- Expanding intervals (1, 3, 7, 21 days) are a common practical schedule; the exact numbers matter less than the growth.

### Mix it up
Doing twenty problems of the same type in a row trains you to recognise the type from context,
not from the problem. Mixing types (interleaving) feels harder and slower during practice but
produces much better results on a mixed test, which is what exams are. The same holds for
subjects: a session of maths, then biology, then maths again beats two blocks.
- Rohrer and Taylor (2007) showed interleaved maths practice roughly doubled performance on a later test compared with blocked practice.
- Interleaving feels worse while you do it; that feeling is not a measure of learning.

### Fluency is a trap
Smooth lectures, highlighted pages and a tidy summary all feel like understanding. They are often
the opposite: the easier something is to take in, the less work your memory does. Good study has
"desirable difficulties": generating the answer before it is shown, explaining a concept in your
own words, drawing the diagram from memory. If it feels easy, you are probably not learning much.
- The term "desirable difficulties" was coined by the psychologist Robert Bjork.
- Highlighting and rereading rank among the least effective techniques in Dunlosky et al.'s 2013 review of ten study methods.

### Errors are information
Getting a question wrong, then seeing the answer, produces more learning than getting it right
the first time, as long as you find out quickly. Trying to answer before you are taught (and
failing) also helps the lesson stick. So quizzes are not just for measuring; they are the
learning. Keep a list of what you got wrong and come back to it; that list is the most valuable
page in your notes.
- Kornell, Hays and Bjork (2009) found that unsuccessful retrieval attempts followed by feedback improved later recall.
- Immediate feedback matters: a wrong answer left uncorrected can be learned as if it were right.

## Facts
- The ten techniques in Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated practice testing and distributed practice as most useful, and highlighting, rereading and summarising as least useful.
- The "forgetting curve" describes how memory fades quickly at first and then more slowly; each retrieval resets it to a slower slope.
- Sleep after study improves consolidation; a session followed by sleep beats the same session followed by a day awake.
- Learning styles (visual, auditory, kinaesthetic) have no experimental support for matching teaching to a preferred style.
