## About
Climate change is the best-measured big problem in the world, and also the most argued about.
These notes stick to what the measurements show and how the physics works: why certain gases
warm the planet, where the extra carbon comes from, what has changed so far, what is projected,
and which responses actually move the numbers. Figures are from the IPCC's sixth assessment
(2021 to 2023) and the Global Carbon Project, rounded for memory.

## Units
### The greenhouse effect
Sunlight warms the Earth's surface; the surface radiates heat back out as infrared. Some gases in
the air (water vapour, carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide) absorb that infrared and re-emit
it in all directions, including back down. Without them the planet would average about minus 18
degrees Celsius instead of about plus 15. The effect itself is natural and was described in the
1800s; the problem is that human activity has raised the concentration of the long-lived gases,
so more heat is trapped.
- Joseph Fourier proposed the atmosphere traps heat in the 1820s; John Tyndall measured which gases absorb infrared in 1859; Svante Arrhenius estimated the warming from doubling CO2 in 1896.
- Water vapour is the largest greenhouse gas but it follows temperature (feedback) rather than driving it.

### Where the carbon comes from
Carbon dioxide in the air has risen from about 280 parts per million before industry to about
420 ppm, higher than at any time in at least 800,000 years of ice-core records. About 90% of
today's emissions come from burning coal, oil and gas; most of the rest from deforestation and
land use. Methane, from livestock, rice, landfill and gas leaks, is shorter-lived but traps far
more heat per molecule. We know the extra carbon is from fossil fuels because its isotope mix
matches ancient plant carbon, and because oxygen in the air is falling in step with combustion.
- Global fossil CO2 emissions are roughly 37 billion tonnes a year (Global Carbon Project, 2023).
- Methane traps about 80 times more heat than CO2 over 20 years, but breaks down in about a decade.

### What has changed already
The global average surface temperature is about 1.1 to 1.2 degrees Celsius above the late 1800s,
with the last ten years the warmest on record. Sea level has risen about 20 centimetres since
1900 and is accelerating, from warming water expanding and from ice loss in Greenland and
Antarctica. Oceans have absorbed most of the extra heat and about a quarter of the CO2, making
them more acidic. Heatwaves, heavy rain and droughts have become more frequent and intense in
most regions, and the IPCC states it is unequivocal that human influence has warmed the planet.
- 2023 was the warmest year in the instrumental record, about 1.45 degrees above the 1850 to 1900 average (WMO).
- Ocean pH has fallen by about 0.1 since pre-industrial times, a roughly 30% increase in acidity.

### What is projected
How warm it gets depends almost entirely on how much more carbon is emitted. Every additional
1,000 billion tonnes of CO2 adds about 0.45 degrees. Current policies point to roughly 2.5 to 3
degrees by 2100; the Paris Agreement goal is to hold warming well below 2 and aim for 1.5. The
difference between 1.5 and 2 is large: twice as many people exposed to severe heat, most versus
nearly all tropical coral reefs lost, and much higher risks to crops in Africa and South Asia.
Reaching net zero CO2 stops warming within a couple of decades; the climate does not keep
warming once emissions stop.
- The Paris Agreement (2015) aims to keep warming well below 2 degrees and pursue 1.5.
- Net zero means emissions are balanced by removals, so the concentration stops rising.

### What actually works
The big levers are known: replace coal and gas electricity with solar, wind, hydro and nuclear;
electrify transport and heating so they run on that clean power; stop deforestation; cut methane
leaks; and use energy more efficiently. Solar and wind are now the cheapest new electricity in
most of the world, which is why they are growing fastest. Individual choices matter most where
they add up: how you travel, what you eat, and what you vote and argue for. Adaptation, such as
drought-resistant crops, flood defences and early-warning systems, is needed whatever happens.
- Solar photovoltaic costs fell about 90% between 2010 and 2020 (IRENA).
- Africa produces under 4% of global fossil CO2 emissions but is among the regions most exposed to the impacts.

## Facts
- Carbon dioxide stays in the atmosphere for centuries; about a fifth of a pulse of CO2 is still there after a thousand years.
- The IPCC is the United Nations body that assesses climate science; its sixth assessment report was published in 2021 to 2023.
- The Keeling Curve, measured at Mauna Loa in Hawaii since 1958, is the longest continuous record of atmospheric CO2.
- Weather is the state of the atmosphere over hours and days; climate is the average over decades.
