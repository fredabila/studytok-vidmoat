## About
Marcus Aurelius was Roman emperor from 161 to 180 and wrote a private notebook, in Greek, that
nobody was meant to read. It survived as the Meditations: short reminders to himself about how
to think, work and treat people when the day is going badly. The ideas are practical enough
that students, athletes and founders still use them. This pack covers the handful of ideas the
book returns to again and again, in plain language, with the situations a student actually meets.

## Units
### What you control and what you do not
The book's first move is a sorting exercise. Some things are up to you: your judgements, your
effort, how you respond. Most things are not: other people's opinions, the weather, exam
questions, the past. Marcus keeps telling himself to put his energy only on the first pile and
to stop being surprised by the second. The practical test is a single question in any situation:
"Which part of this is mine?" A student who fails a test cannot change the mark, but can change
how they revise tomorrow.
- Marcus Aurelius wrote the Meditations for himself, not for publication, around 170 to 180 CE.
- The division between what is "up to us" and "not up to us" comes from the Stoic school, founded in Athens around 300 BCE by Zeno of Citium.

### The obstacle becomes the way
One of the most quoted lines says that what stands in the way of an action can itself become
the action. The idea is not that problems are good, but that every obstacle gives you something
to practise: patience when a bus is late, honesty when a mistake is found, courage when a result
is bad. Marcus treats difficulty as the raw material of character, the way a fire uses whatever
is thrown into it. For a student this reframes a hard topic from "this is blocking me" to "this
is the thing I am here to get better at".
- The line is in Book 5 of the Meditations: the impediment to action advances action; what stands in the way becomes the way.

### The morning reminder
Marcus begins one entry by telling himself that during the day he will meet people who are
rude, ungrateful and selfish, and that none of it should surprise him, because they act that
way out of not knowing better, and because he and they are made for cooperation like the two
rows of teeth. The point is not cynicism. It is to decide in advance how you will react, so the
first annoying person of the day does not set your mood. Students use the same trick before a
group project or a difficult class.
- The "two rows of teeth" comparison is from Book 2, section 1.

### Memento mori, used well
The book keeps returning to the fact that everyone, including emperors, is forgotten soon. Marcus
does not say this to be gloomy; he uses it to shrink worries down to their real size. Will this
embarrassment matter in a year? Will anyone remember this argument? The exercise clears space
for what does matter: doing the work in front of you well, and being decent to the people
around you. It is a tool for priorities, not a mood.
- Marcus Aurelius died in 180 CE, probably at Vindobona (Vienna) or Sirmium, during a military campaign.

### Act, do not talk about acting
A repeated reminder: stop arguing about what a good person is like, and be one. Marcus is
suspicious of his own tendency to philosophise instead of doing. His cure is small and
immediate: the next thing in front of you, done properly, without complaining and without an
audience. Students recognise this as the difference between planning a study schedule and
opening the book.

## Facts
- Marcus Aurelius was Roman emperor from 161 to 180 CE and co-ruled with Lucius Verus until 169.
- The Meditations is written in Koine Greek in twelve books, mostly short numbered entries.
- Stoicism teaches that the only true good is virtue (wisdom, justice, courage, self-control) and that externals are "indifferent".
- The earliest surviving complete manuscript dates from the 14th century; the first printed edition appeared in 1559.
- The English title "Meditations" is modern; the Greek manuscripts call it "To himself" (Ta eis heauton).
